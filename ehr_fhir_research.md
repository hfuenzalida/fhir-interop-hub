# End-to-End Landscape of Electronic Health Records (EHRs), Social Feedback, and HL7 FHIR for Software Design

## Executive Summary

Electronic Health Records (EHRs) have evolved from isolated, proprietary systems into a highly networked, standards‑driven ecosystem that must balance clinical workflow, patient engagement, regulatory compliance, and large‑scale interoperability.[web:1][web:11] Modern EHR software must not only store structured clinical data but also integrate with health information exchanges (HIEs), patient portals, telehealth platforms, and analytical tools while complying with privacy and security regulations.[web:12][web:16] HL7 FHIR (Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources) has emerged as the de facto modern standard for API‑based health data exchange, providing a resource‑oriented model, RESTful APIs, and a rich ecosystem of implementation guides and tools.[web:14][web:17][web:23] At the same time, clinician and patient feedback — often captured in academic studies, usability research, and social media analyses — highlights major pain points around usability, workflow misalignment, and burnout that should directly inform new EHR designs.[web:18][web:32][web:37]

This report synthesizes evidence from web, academic, and social‑media‑oriented research on EHRs, integrates a focused deep‑dive into HL7 FHIR, and translates these insights into a concrete, high‑level software architecture blueprint for a modern FHIR‑native EHR platform.

## 1. EHR Concepts, Value Proposition, and Ecosystem

### 1.1 Core definition and scope

Electronic Health Records are longitudinal, digital records of patient health information generated by one or more encounters in any care setting, including demographics, problem lists, medications, allergies, immunizations, laboratory results, imaging, and clinical notes.[web:11][web:16] Unlike simple electronic medical records (EMRs), EHRs are explicitly designed to support data sharing across organizations and settings, enabling coordinated care and analytics.[web:1][web:9] Modern EHR ecosystems also include ancillary modules (billing, scheduling, order entry, decision support) and external applications integrated via APIs, health information exchanges, and patient portals.[web:5][web:16]

### 1.2 Benefits and positive impacts

Systematic reviews report that EHR adoption can improve quality and safety by making information more accessible, reducing medication errors, and enabling decision support.[web:2][web:16] Patient access to EHRs through portals is associated with better treatment adherence, self‑management, communication with providers, and patient satisfaction.[web:15][web:30] Qualitative studies with physicians indicate that EHRs improve legibility, data availability, and remote access, which practitioners view as core benefits despite the burden.[web:28][web:40]

### 1.3 Persistent challenges and harms

Despite benefits, lack of interoperability between heterogeneous EHR systems leads to data silos, duplicate testing, and incomplete information at the point of care.[web:9][web:11] Usability problems — excessive clicks, poor information architecture, alert fatigue, and documentation burden — contribute to clinician burnout and can be linked to patient safety risks.[web:18][web:32][web:35] Studies show a strong association between low EHR usability scores and higher odds of physician burnout, with many clinicians explicitly naming EHRs as a primary source of stress.[web:34][web:37][web:43]

## 2. Interoperability Standards for EHRs

### 2.1 Interoperability levels

A scoping review of EHR interoperability identifies four practically visible levels: technical/foundational (basic connectivity), syntactic/structural (common formats), semantic (shared meaning via terminologies), and process/organizational (alignment of workflows and responsibilities).[web:9][web:22] While technical and structural interoperability are increasingly attainable, semantic interoperability — preserving clinical meaning across systems — remains the main unsolved challenge.[web:3][web:9] Standards must therefore cover data models, transport protocols, terminologies (SNOMED CT, LOINC, ICD‑10), and higher‑level process agreements.[web:3][web:13]

### 2.2 Major standards (HL7 v2, CDA, openEHR, DICOM, etc.)

Traditional HL7 Version 2 messaging supports event‑driven data exchange (ADTs, orders, results) in hospitals and remains heavily used for interfacing legacy systems.[web:1][web:13] HL7 Clinical Document Architecture (CDA) and Continuity of Care Document (CCD) provide XML‑based document structures for clinical summaries and are widely used in HIEs.[web:8][web:47] openEHR defines a multi‑layer information model with archetypes and templates for semantically rich, vendor‑independent clinical data storage.[web:8][web:69] DICOM standardizes medical imaging formats and workflows, enabling image exchange between PACS and EHR systems.[web:11][web:13]

### 2.3 Regulatory and regional frameworks

The European Health Data Space (EHDS) establishes mandatory interoperability requirements for EHR systems, including harmonized datasets, coding systems, and an EU‑wide EHR exchange format (EEHRxF) for cross‑border care.[web:12] EHR systems in the EU will be required to implement an interoperability component capable of import/export using the EEHRxF and a standardized logging component for access auditing.[web:12] In the United States, the ONC Cures Act Final Rule promotes API‑based access and prohibits information blocking, driving adoption of FHIR‑based APIs for certified health IT.[web:58][web:62]

## 3. HL7 FHIR: Standard, Architecture, and Ecosystem

### 3.1 FHIR overview and design principles

HL7 FHIR is a health information exchange standard by HL7 International that defines data formats ("resources") and a RESTful API for interoperable EHR data exchange.[web:14][web:23] FHIR combines lessons from HL7 v2, v3, and CDA with modern web technologies such as REST, JSON, and XML to simplify implementation and support mobile and cloud applications.[web:14][web:56] Its design follows an 80/20 rule: the base specification covers roughly 80% of common use cases using standard resources, with controlled extensibility for the remaining 20%.[web:20][web:23]

### 3.2 Resource‑based data model

FHIR models healthcare concepts as modular "resources" like Patient, Practitioner, Encounter, Observation, Condition, MedicationRequest, and DocumentReference, each with defined elements, data types, and relationships.[web:14][web:23][web:56] Resources are intended to be composable, reusable building blocks that map directly to on‑the‑wire representations in JSON or XML.[web:14][web:56] Implementation guides and profiles constrain and extend base resources for specific domains (for example, oncology mCODE, pathology reports with images, and FHIR‑based EHR functional models).[web:48][web:51][web:59]

### 3.3 FHIR modules and functional domains

The FHIR specification organizes resources into modules such as Foundation, Base Administration (Patient, Practitioner, Organization), Clinical (CarePlan, DiagnosticReport), Financial (Claim, Coverage), and Clinical Reasoning (Measure, PlanDefinition, Library).[web:56][web:23] This modular structure allows implementers to select relevant subsets for their use case — for example, an EHR core that uses administrative and clinical modules plus Clinical Reasoning for decision support.[web:56] The EHR‑S FM implementation based on FHIR Requirements resources demonstrates how functional requirements can be linked directly to FHIR resource structures.[web:59]

### 3.4 APIs, operations, and SMART on FHIR

FHIR defines RESTful interactions (Create, Read, Update, Delete, Search) on resources via predictable URLs such as `[base]/Patient/{id}` and query parameters for search.[web:14][web:56][web:61] SMART on FHIR adds a layer specifying OAuth 2.0‑based authorization, launch contexts, and scopes so that substitutable apps can securely run inside or alongside EHRs.[web:17][web:61][web:67] Cloud platforms and EHR vendors expose FHIR endpoints with SMART on FHIR access controls, enabling third‑party apps to retrieve and write EHR data for authorized patients and users.[web:64][web:67]

### 3.5 Implementation guides and tooling

The FHIR ecosystem includes a registry of implementation guides (IGs) that profile resources for specific regions, programs, and clinical domains.[web:57][web:68] Examples include oncology (mCODE), emergency reporting (SANER), pathology reporting, and national EHR programs; these IGs provide conformance profiles, value sets, and architectural patterns.[web:48][web:51][web:55] Tooling such as validators, reference servers, test harnesses, and implementation checklists from bodies like HL7 and CDC support consistent, standards‑conformant implementations.[web:58][web:62]

## 4. Academic and Social‑Feedback Insights on EHR Use

### 4.1 Clinician usability and burnout

Large survey and literature reviews show that EHR usability is often rated poor, with average System Usability Scale (SUS) scores falling in the "unacceptable" range compared to other industries.[web:18][web:34] Burnout is strongly associated with perceived EHR burden, especially documentation time, alert overload, and after‑hours work interacting with the EHR.[web:37][web:40][web:43] Specific usability flaws such as excessive navigation, inconsistent icons, and redundant data entry increase cognitive load, documentation errors, and safety risks.[web:32][web:35][web:42]

### 4.2 Patient experience, portals, and engagement

Systematic reviews report that patient access to EHRs through portals improves engagement, adherence, communication, and in some cases health outcomes, though barriers such as usability issues and digital literacy persist.[web:15][web:21][web:30] Surveys of national patient portals find generally positive usability ratings but highlight specific pain points, such as difficulties finding information, understanding terminology, and navigating complex interfaces.[web:30] Telehealth systems integrated with EHRs are viewed positively by patients for convenience and continuity, yet face barriers around technical reliability, integration across care sites, and billing/insurance workflows.[web:21]

### 4.3 Complaints, safety, and harm signals

Studies analyzing patient safety reports and incident data link EHR usability issues (data entry, alerting, interoperability, visual display) to adverse events, particularly around medication ordering and administration.[web:35][web:36] Content analyses of patient complaints (letters and online platforms) identify hot spots such as delays in access, examination quality, care continuity, and respect/patient rights, many of which intersect with information flow and EHR‑enabled processes.[web:24][web:26][web:31] Methodologies like the Healthcare Complaints Analysis Tool (HCAT) and its general practice adaptation demonstrate how structured complaint data can identify systemic hot spots and blind spots along the care pathway.[web:26][web:31]

### 4.4 Social networks, peer influence, and adoption

Social network analysis of physicians shows that peer influence in friendship networks affects EHR adoption and usage intensity more than formal professional consultation networks.[web:38] Informal champions and opinion leaders within a medical group can accelerate or hinder EHR uptake depending on their attitudes and experiences.[web:38] This suggests that change‑management strategies for new EHR platforms should explicitly model and leverage social network structures within organizations.

### 4.5 Social media and online discourse around EHRs

Studies mining large‑scale online content (such as YouTube tutorials and Twitter posts) demonstrate the feasibility of extracting usability themes, common tasks, and misconceptions about EHR systems.[web:25][web:39] Patient and clinician sentiment analyses on social platforms highlight recurring concerns about complexity, time burden, and perceived abstraction away from patient care, but also showcase tips, workarounds, and training resources that improve workflow integration.[web:25][web:39][web:44] These findings imply that a feedback‑driven EHR roadmap should continuously ingest and analyze open‑web and social signals as part of product discovery.

## 5. HL7 FHIR in Real‑World Architectures and Open‑Source EHRs

### 5.1 Reference architectures and integration patterns

Recent implementation blueprints describe combining legacy HL7 v2, CDA/CCD, and FHIR APIs via integration engines and middleware to provide end‑to‑end clinical data interoperability.[web:47][web:65] Typical reference architectures feature FHIR servers as central interoperability hubs, surrounded by adapters that translate from legacy messages, ETL pipelines that normalize codes, and security layers enforcing consent and audit requirements.[web:49][web:56] Event‑driven and microservices architectures using containerization, message brokers, and scalable cloud storage have been validated for FHIR‑based platforms supporting thousands of concurrent users.[web:49][web:50]

### 5.2 FHIR in IoMT and real‑time data

Open‑source frameworks demonstrate how FHIR Observations can be used to standardize real‑time streaming of biomedical signals from distributed sensors into monitoring dashboards.[web:50] Such frameworks combine microcontrollers, MQTT transport, and time‑series databases with FHIR‑compliant interfaces, achieving low latency and semantic interoperability without proprietary middleware.[web:50] This pattern can be extended to home monitoring, wearables, and ICU telemetry, enabling continuous EHR enrichment.

### 5.3 SMART on FHIR apps and data hubs

SMART on FHIR implementations have used intermediary data hubs that store patient‑reported outcomes (PROs) and expose them back to multiple EHRs via FHIR APIs, reducing the need for deeply customized EHR schema changes.[web:64] This architecture uses FHIR as the canonical data format and SMART for authentication, with web‑based components embedded in EHR UIs to present visualizations and questionnaires.[web:64][web:61] Cloud health APIs such as Google Cloud Healthcare implement SMART on FHIR access enforcement and scope‑based authorization to control app access to EHR data.[web:67]

### 5.4 Bridging FHIR with openEHR and other models

Engines such as openFHIR and research frameworks like FHIRconnect provide bidirectional mappings between openEHR archetype‑based repositories and FHIR resources, enabling robust storage with FHIR‑based access APIs.[web:60][web:66] These mapping frameworks use domain‑specific languages and reusable archetype‑to‑resource mapping libraries to reduce custom ETL work and promote semantic consistency.[web:66] Such approaches are particularly relevant for long‑term clinical data repositories that must support both rich modeling (openEHR) and broad interoperability (FHIR).

### 5.5 FHIR‑native and open‑source EHR platforms

Emerging FHIR‑native EHR projects provide open‑source platforms where the primary data model and API surface are FHIR resources rather than internal proprietary schemas.[web:63][web:52] These systems often expose modular, configurable components that can be adapted to specific workflows, representing a shift toward composable, standards‑first EHR architectures.[web:63] They also demonstrate patterns for integrating role‑specific portals, microservices, and AI‑enhanced components using FHIR as the substrate.[web:53][web:49]

## 6. Design Requirements for a Modern FHIR‑Native EHR Platform

### 6.1 Functional requirements

A modern EHR should support core clinical workflows: patient registration, scheduling, documentation, order entry, medication management, results review, and discharge/transfer processes.[web:16][web:28] It must also provide patient‑facing capabilities such as portals, messaging, telehealth, and record download/sharing in interoperable formats.[web:15][web:21][web:30] Decision support (alerts, reminders, clinical pathways), population health reporting, and quality measures are critical for contemporary care delivery and reimbursement models.[web:2][web:16]

### 6.2 Non‑functional requirements

High usability is essential to reduce cognitive load, documentation burden, and burnout, requiring thoughtful information architecture, minimized clicks, and contextualization of alerts.[web:18][web:32][web:37] Reliability, performance, and horizontal scalability are mandatory for hospital‑scale deployment, demonstrated by load‑testing studies of FHIR platforms supporting thousands of concurrent users.[web:49][web:50] Security, privacy, and compliance (HIPAA/GDPR/EHDS, audit logs, consent management, and least‑privilege access) must be integrated into architecture, not bolted on.[web:12][web:58]

### 6.3 Interoperability and standards alignment

The platform must implement FHIR R4/R5 resources and key profiles relevant to target markets (for example, US Core, national EHR profiles, or EHDS EEHRxF mappings).[web:57][web:12] Legacy integration support for HL7 v2, CDA/CCD, and DICOM is necessary to connect with existing hospital systems and imaging archives.[web:47][web:11][web:13] Terminology services should support SNOMED CT, LOINC, ICD‑10, and local extensions, ensuring semantic interoperability and consistent analytics.[web:3][web:13]

### 6.4 UX principles informed by evidence and social feedback

Interface design should minimize navigation friction, redundant data entry, and disruptive alerts, prioritizing key workflows identified as hot spots in usability and burnout studies.[web:32][web:35][web:37] Dashboards and documentation interfaces should support summarization and "big picture" views to counteract fragmentation and information overload described by clinicians.[web:32][web:40][web:43] Continuous feedback loops — via in‑product telemetry, surveys, and mining of public sentiment — should feed into an iterative design and improvement process.

## 7. Target Architecture for a FHIR‑Native EHR Software Platform

### 7.1 High‑level component architecture

A plausible high‑level architecture for a FHIR‑native EHR platform comprises the following layers:

- Presentation layer: web and mobile clients for clinicians, patients, and administrators, plus embedded SMART on FHIR apps within partner EHRs.[web:17][web:61][web:63]
- API and interoperability layer: one or more FHIR servers exposing RESTful endpoints, SMART on FHIR authorization, and integration APIs for external systems.[web:49][web:56][web:67]
- Clinical services layer: domain microservices handling scheduling, orders, documentation, decision support, messaging, and reporting, backed by the FHIR data model.[web:49][web:53]
- Data storage and persistence: transactional stores for FHIR resources (document or relational), specialized stores for time‑series and documents, and data warehouses/lakes for analytics.[web:49][web:50]
- Integration and transformation layer: interface engines that translate HL7 v2, CDA, openEHR, IoMT data, and other formats into FHIR resources using mapping frameworks like openFHIR/FHIRconnect.[web:47][web:60][web:66]
- Security and governance layer: identity and access management (for example, Keycloak), OAuth 2.0/OpenID Connect, consent management, audit logging, and compliance reporting.[web:49][web:58]

### 7.2 FHIR server and data model design

The FHIR server should support CRUD and search operations for core resources (Patient, Encounter, Observation, Condition, MedicationRequest, Procedure, DocumentReference, CarePlan, Appointment, Practitioner, Organization).[web:14][web:23][web:56] Profiles must be defined for site‑specific or program‑specific constraints (for example, required identifiers, value sets) using implementation guide tooling.[web:57][web:59] Data mapping pipelines should align internal clinical models or openEHR archetypes with FHIR resources to avoid duplication and ensure a canonical representation.[web:60][web:66]

### 7.3 SMART on FHIR and identity management

User authentication and authorization can rely on OAuth 2.0 with SMART on FHIR scopes, supporting patient, user, and system contexts for different interaction patterns.[web:61][web:67] An identity provider (such as Keycloak) manages users, roles (clinician, admin, patient), and client applications, issuing tokens that encode FHIR scopes and patient context.[web:49][web:67] Launch workflows must support embedded apps inside other EHRs as well as standalone access, aligning with SMART and vendor‑specific launch requirements.[web:17][web:64]

### 7.4 Integration with legacy systems and HIEs

An integration engine should ingest HL7 v2 messages, CDA/CCD documents, and national EHR exchange formats, transforming them into FHIR resources and handling acknowledgments and error management.[web:47][web:65] Where openEHR repositories exist, mapping engines (openFHIR/FHIRconnect) can provide bidirectional synchronization between archetype‑based storage and FHIR‑based APIs.[web:60][web:66] Connectivity to regional and national HIEs should follow relevant IGs and EEHRxF/US Core constraints, supporting both push (subscriptions, notifications) and pull (query‑based) models.[web:12][web:57]

### 7.5 IoMT, telemetry, and real‑time streams

Edge and gateway components can receive IoMT data via lightweight protocols such as MQTT, transform them into FHIR Observations, and forward them to the central FHIR server or data hub.[web:50] Time‑series databases and streaming analytics engines can process high‑frequency signals while persisting clinically relevant summaries and alerts as FHIR resources.[web:50][web:49] These capabilities should be configurable per program (for example, chronic disease remote monitoring vs. acute hospital telemetry).

### 7.6 Analytics, decision support, and AI integration

A data warehouse or lake fed from the FHIR store can support analytics, reporting, and ML model training for population health and operational intelligence.[web:2][web:16] Clinical decision support can leverage FHIR’s Clinical Reasoning module and standard artifacts such as Measure and PlanDefinition, with rules and ML models consuming FHIR resources as inputs.[web:56][web:59] Emerging AI components (for example, LLM‑based triage assistants) can interact with the EHR via FHIR APIs in a governed way, as demonstrated by architectures for AI‑powered health assistants.[web:53]

## 8. Development Roadmap and Key Technical Decisions

### 8.1 Technology stack considerations

Evidence from scalable FHIR platform implementations supports microservices deployed in containers (for example, Kubernetes) with API gateways and service meshes for observability and security.[web:49][web:54] Open‑source FHIR servers and EHR frameworks (such as FHIR‑native EHR projects and openFHIR) can accelerate development by providing core resource handling and mapping engines.[web:60][web:63][web:66] The choice of programming languages and frameworks should prioritize robust ecosystem support for FHIR libraries, security stacks, and cloud‑native patterns.

### 8.2 Standards and conformance strategy

The project should align with specific implementation guides and certification frameworks for target markets (US Core, EHDS EEHRxF, local national profiles) from the outset.[web:12][web:57][web:62] Continuous conformance testing using HL7 validators, public test harnesses, and vendor test beds (for example, Touchstone) reduces integration friction with external EHRs and payers.[web:56][web:58] Governance processes must control the introduction of new profiles, extensions, and mappings to avoid uncontrolled divergence.

### 8.3 UX, safety, and burnout mitigation strategy

Usability testing should focus on high‑risk workflows identified in burnout and safety research — order entry, medication reconciliation, documentation, alerts, and inbox management.[web:32][web:35][web:37] The product roadmap should include explicit goals to reduce documentation time, limit unnecessary alerts, and support summarized visualizations that aid clinicians in seeing the "big picture".[web:18][web:32][web:40] Integration of structured feedback from patient complaints, portal surveys, and social‑media monitoring can help identify emerging problems before they manifest as safety incidents.

### 8.4 Data governance, security, and compliance

Architectural decisions must embed privacy‑by‑design and security‑by‑design principles, including encryption in transit and at rest, fine‑grained access control, and comprehensive audit logging.[web:12][web:58] Consent management should reflect local regulations, allowing patients to control data sharing with other providers, apps, and research initiatives.[web:12][web:62] Regular security assessments, threat modeling, and adherence to secure coding practices are necessary given the sensitivity and regulatory scrutiny of EHR data.

## 9. From Research to Implementation: Using This Report as Design Input

The literature and web evidence reviewed here converge on several design imperatives for a next‑generation EHR platform:

- Use FHIR as the primary interoperability and API model, complemented by robust mapping to legacy standards and openEHR where appropriate.[web:47][web:56][web:60]
- Prioritize usability and workflow alignment to reduce clinician burden and burnout, guided by empirical findings from usability and burnout research.[web:32][web:37][web:43]
- Treat patient access, portals, and telehealth as first‑class components that drive engagement and outcomes, not add‑on features.[web:15][web:21][web:30]
- Build on microservices‑ and cloud‑native architectures validated in recent FHIR platform research for scalability, security, and flexibility.[web:49][web:50][web:54]
- Establish a strong conformance and governance strategy for standards, profiles, and terminology to maintain semantic interoperability and certification readiness.[web:12][web:57][web:62]

These principles, combined with the component architecture described above, provide a concrete foundation for designing a complete software platform for medical records that is standards‑aligned, evidence‑informed, and responsive to real‑world clinician and patient needs.
